# Taste Lens: Ranking Novel Opening Sentences

A reusable rubric for judging the first sentence of a novel. Score each criterion, then apply the ordering principle to break ties. The aim is to separate the genuinely excellent from the merely competent or the merely vivid.

## Criteria

1. **Earned specificity** — Reward a detail so particular it could only belong to this story and implies a world beyond itself; penalize decorative or generic detail chosen to sound "literary" rather than to mean something.

2. **Implication over explanation** — Reward a line that plants a question, tension, or gap the reader must lean in to close; penalize a line that hands over its situation, mood, or theme fully explained, leaving nothing to pull against.

3. **Live consciousness** — Reward the felt presence of a mind with a stance, voice, or way of seeing — someone is perceiving, not just a camera; penalize inert scene-setting or neutral reportage with no one behind the eyes.

4. **Structural work** — Reward an image or fact that does load-bearing work (establishing stakes, character, or the book's governing logic) so it pays off later; penalize the beautiful ornament that could be deleted without loss.

5. **Cadence** — Reward rhythm and sentence shape that enact meaning — where the placement of the final word or a controlled turn lands with intent; penalize flat, arrhythmic phrasing or music laid on for its own sake.

6. **Restraint / calibrated reach** — Reward a line that attempts exactly as much as it can deliver and trusts the reader; penalize over-reaching (straining for profundity, piling on adjectives, announcing significance) and under-reaching (safe, forgettable throat-clearing).

7. **Compression** — Reward maximum consequence per word, where a short span opens a large space; penalize slack, redundancy, and words that neither turn nor tighten.

8. **Trajectory** — Reward a line that creates forward pressure — a reason the next sentence must exist; penalize the self-contained aphorism or static tableau that could just as easily be an ending.

## Ordering principle

When two openings tie, prefer the one that opens the larger world with the fewer, truer words — the line you cannot stop thinking about over the line you merely admired.
